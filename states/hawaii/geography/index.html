HIHawaii Geography

Capital: Honolulu · West · Admitted 1959

The Pacific Plate creeps northwest at roughly three to four inches a year. The volcanic hotspot underneath it does not move at all. Almost every feature of Hawaii follows from that single arrangement. Kauaʻi, at the far northwest end of the inhabited chain, last erupted around five million years ago and has been dissolving into canyons and cliffs ever since. Hawaiʻi Island sits about 350 miles southeast, is younger than a million years old, and is still under construction.

That makes Hawaii the only state not resting on the North American Plate, and the only one whose land area changes on a schedule set by eruptions rather than by surveyors.

Islands that get older toward the northwest

Run a finger up the chain and the ages line up in order. Kauaʻi and Niʻihau are the eroded elders. Oʻahu, built by the extinct Waiʻanae and Koʻolau shields, comes next. Then Molokaʻi, Lānaʻi, Maui and Kahoʻolawe, which once formed a single landmass geologists call Maui Nui before rising seas cut it into pieces. Hawaiʻi Island anchors the young end, assembled from five overlapping volcanoes: Kohala, Mauna Kea, Hualālai, Mauna Loa and Kīlauea.

The next island in the sequence already exists. Kamaʻehuakanaloa, the seamount off the southeast coast renamed from Lōʻihi in 2021, tops out roughly 3,000 feet below the surface and will need tens of thousands of years before it breaks into daylight.

Kīlauea's 2018 lower Puna eruption showed how fast the accounting can change. Lava buried Kapoho Bay and the Vacationland subdivision and built about 875 acres of brand new coastline. At the same time the summit caldera collapsed inward, dropping hundreds of feet over three months of near daily explosions. Mauna Loa, quiet since 1984, reopened in November 2022.

Mauna Kea depends on where you start measuring

Mauna Kea reaches 13,803 feet above sea level, which makes it the highest point in the state and puts snow on a tropical summit most winters. Measured from its base on the ocean floor, though, the mountain stands more than 33,000 feet tall, exceeding Everest by any base to summit reckoning. Mauna Loa is 125 feet shorter at 13,678 feet but far more massive, holding more rock than any other single volcano on the planet.

Near Mauna Kea's summit sits Lake Waiau at about 13,020 feet, one of the highest lakes in the United States and, in a state with almost no standing fresh water, a genuine oddity. The state's lowest point is sea level, so Hawaii's total relief runs the full 13,803 feet in a land area of only 10,932 square miles, ranked 43rd.

Eight main islands and 1,400 miles beyond them

The eight islands people mean when they say Hawaii are Hawaiʻi, Maui, Oʻahu, Kauaʻi, Molokaʻi, Lānaʻi, Niʻihau and Kahoʻolawe. Kahoʻolawe has no permanent residents and served as a Navy bombing range until 1990. Niʻihau has been privately held since 1864.

The archipelago does not stop there. Northwest of Kauaʻi the chain continues for about 1,400 more miles as reefs, atolls and rock stacks out to Kure Atoll, roughly 137 islands and islets in total. Papahānaumokuākea Marine National Monument covers that stretch, and after its 2016 expansion it protects 582,578 square miles of ocean.

Ka Lae, the wind hammered basalt point at the southern tip of Hawaiʻi Island, is the southernmost land in the fifty states. Hawaii shares no border with anyone, keeps its own clock at Hawaii Aleutian Standard Time, and skips daylight saving entirely, which leaves it five hours behind New York in January and six hours behind in July.

Soaked on one flank, parched on the other

Northeast trade winds hit the mountains, rise, cool and dump their moisture on the windward side. What crosses the crest arrives dry. The result is rainfall gradients no other state can match over such short distances. Hilo, on the windward coast of Hawaiʻi Island, collects more than 120 inches a year. The leeward Kohala coast, a couple of hours away by car, records under ten.

Mount Waiʻaleʻale on Kauaʻi, 5,148 feet high and almost permanently clouded, averages roughly 450 inches annually and is regularly ranked among the wettest measured spots on Earth. That water carved Waimea Canyon, about ten miles long and more than 3,000 feet deep, into the island's western flank.

Temperature records reflect the same compressed geography. The state high is 100 degrees, set at Pahala in April 1931. The state low is 12 degrees, recorded at the Mauna Kea Observatory in May 1979.

One navigable river and 750 miles of shoreline

Young volcanic rock drains too well to hold big rivers, so Hawaii has essentially one that boats can use: the Wailua on Kauaʻi, the oldest and most eroded of the main islands. Streams elsewhere run steep and short, plunging over sea cliffs rather than meandering to a delta.

Coastline is the compensation. Hawaii has about 750 miles of it, fourth among the states behind Alaska, Florida and California, and it ranges from black sand beaches to the sea cliffs along Molokaʻi's north shore, which rise more than 3,000 feet and rank among the tallest in the world. The Alenuihāhā Channel between Maui and Hawaiʻi Island drops to roughly 6,000 feet and produces some of the roughest crossing conditions in the Pacific.

Frequently asked questions

What is the highest point in Hawaii?

Mauna Kea on Hawaii Island is the highest point in Hawaii at 13,803 feet above sea level. Measured from its base on the ocean floor it rises more than 33,000 feet, more than Mount Everest measured base to summit.

How many islands does Hawaii have?

Hawaii has eight main islands: Hawaii, Maui, Oahu, Kauai, Molokai, Lanai, Niihau and Kahoolawe. Counting the reefs, atolls and islets of the Northwestern Hawaiian Islands, the archipelago includes roughly 137 islands stretching about 1,500 miles.

Why are the Hawaiian islands different ages?

The Pacific Plate moves northwest over a fixed volcanic hotspot at about three to four inches per year. Islands form above the hotspot then drift away and erode, so Kauai at the northwest end is about five million years old while Hawaii Island at the southeast end is under one million years old and still erupting.

Does Hawaii have rivers?

Hawaii has streams on every island but only one navigable river, the Wailua on Kauai. Young volcanic rock is too porous to sustain large river systems, so most Hawaiian streams are short and steep.

What is the wettest place in Hawaii?

Mount Waialeale on Kauai averages roughly 450 inches of rain a year and is regularly listed among the wettest measured places on Earth. On the leeward Kohala coast of Hawaii Island, rainfall drops below ten inches a year.
