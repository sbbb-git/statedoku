The rift that is pulling the state apart

The Rio Grande does not run down the middle of New Mexico by accident. It runs down a crack. The Rio Grande Rift is an active continental rift, a zone where the crust is being stretched thin and dropped in blocks, and it reaches from central Colorado through the whole length of New Mexico and past El Paso into Chihuahua. Albuquerque sits in one of its deepest basins, where more than two miles of sediment have filled the trough. The Sandia Mountains east of the city are the rift's uplifted shoulder, which is why they present a granite face rising roughly a mile above downtown.

Rifting also explains how much young volcanic rock the state carries. The Valles Caldera in the Jemez Mountains is a bowl about 13 miles across, left by an eruption roughly 1.25 million years ago. The Carrizozo Malpais, a black basalt flow running some 40 miles down the Tularosa Basin, is only about 5,000 years old and still looks freshly poured. Shiprock in the northwest is the opposite case: a volcanic throat whose surrounding cone eroded away, leaving a 1,583 foot spire of hardened magma standing alone on the Navajo Nation.

Wheeler Peak down to Red Bluff

The high point is Wheeler Peak at 13,161 feet, in the Sangre de Cristo range north of Taos. The low point is Red Bluff Reservoir on the Pecos River at 2,842 feet, sitting exactly on the Texas line in Eddy County. Only Colorado and Wyoming have a lowest point higher than New Mexico's. Mean elevation across the state is roughly 5,700 feet, fourth highest of the fifty. Santa Fe, at 7,199 feet, is both the highest state capital in the country and the oldest, laid out in 1610.

Four major physiographic provinces meet inside the state lines: the Southern Rocky Mountains in the north, the Colorado Plateau in the northwest, the Basin and Range in the southwest, and the Great Plains in the east. The eastern quarter is dominated by the Llano Estacado, the Staked Plains, whose western edge is a caprock escarpment several hundred feet high running north to south. Stacking that much elevation produces six distinct life zones, from Lower Sonoran desert scrub along the southern Rio Grande below about 4,500 feet to alpine tundra above timberline on Wheeler Peak.

Three seas, and basins that reach none of them

Water leaves New Mexico in three directions. The Rio Grande and its tributary the Pecos run to the Gulf of Mexico directly. The Canadian River in the northeast joins the Arkansas, then the Mississippi, reaching the same gulf by a far longer route. The San Juan in the northwest and the Gila in the southwest both feed the Colorado River and end up in the Gulf of California. The Continental Divide crosses the western third of the state.

Some New Mexico water never leaves at all. The Tularosa Basin, the Estancia Basin and the Plains of San Agustin are closed basins with no outlet, where rainfall evaporates where it lands. White Sands is what that produces. Gypsum washed off the Sacramento and San Andres mountains collects in Lake Lucero, dries into selenite crystals, and gets ground by the wind into sand, building the largest gypsum dune field on earth at roughly 275 square miles. It was made the country's 62nd national park in December 2019.

Caves dissolved by acid, not rainwater

The Guadalupe Mountains in the southeast are not folded or faulted mountains. They are the exposed remains of the Capitan Reef, a barrier reef that grew around a Permian sea about 265 million years ago and was then buried, lifted and stripped bare. The caves inside them were not made the usual way. Rather than rainwater carrying weak carbonic acid down from above, hydrogen sulfide seeping up from the oil and gas of the Delaware Basin met oxygenated groundwater and formed sulfuric acid, which ate the limestone from below and left thick gypsum deposits behind as evidence.

The Big Room at Carlsbad Caverns covers about 8.2 acres in a single chamber. Nearby Lechuguilla Cave, entered in 1986 after diggers cleared a rubble-choked pit, has more than 150 miles of surveyed passage and bottoms out 1,604 feet down, making it the deepest limestone cave in the United States.

Straight lines, one bootheel, one corner

Most of New Mexico's outline is survey work: the 37th parallel across the top, the 103rd meridian down much of the east, the 109th down the west. The exception is the bootheel in the southwest, where the southern boundary runs west from the Rio Grande along one parallel, drops south, then continues west along a lower one, hanging a block of land below the rest of the state. That block arrived with the Gadsden Purchase, signed in December 1853 and ratified the following year, bought so that a southern railroad to the Pacific could cross low ground instead of mountains.

The northwest corner is Four Corners, the only point in the country where four states meet. The bronze marker is not quite where the 109th meridian was supposed to fall; an 1875 survey placed it a few hundred feet east of the intended line. Courts have consistently held that the original monument, not the theoretical coordinate, defines the legal corner, so the mistake is now the boundary.

One clock, and a 172 degree swing

New Mexico is entirely on Mountain Time and observes daylight saving. Arizona next door does not, so for roughly eight months of the year the clocks in Gallup and Flagstaff differ by an hour, and for the other four they agree.

The state record high is 122 °F, recorded near Loving on 27 June 1994. The record low is,50 °F at Gavilan on 1 February 1951. The 172 degree gap between them is a product of thin, dry air at altitude, which absorbs sunlight quickly and then radiates the heat away just as fast once the sun is down.

Frequently asked questions

What is the highest point in New Mexico?

Wheeler Peak, 13,161 feet, in the Sangre de Cristo Mountains north of Taos. The lowest point is Red Bluff Reservoir on the Pecos River at 2,842 feet, on the Texas line in Eddy County. Only Colorado and Wyoming have a higher low point than New Mexico.

What are the main geographic regions of New Mexico?

Four physiographic provinces meet inside the state: the Southern Rocky Mountains in the north, the Colorado Plateau in the northwest, the Basin and Range in the southwest, and the Great Plains in the east, where the Llano Estacado ends at a caprock escarpment. The Rio Grande Rift runs north to south through the middle of all of them.

Why is the sand at White Sands white?

It is gypsum rather than quartz. Gypsum washes off the surrounding mountains into the Tularosa Basin, which has no outlet, dries at Lake Lucero into selenite crystals, and is ground by wind into pale sand. The result is the largest gypsum dune field on earth, about 275 square miles, made a national park in December 2019.

Is all of New Mexico in the same time zone?

Yes, the whole state observes Mountain Time and it uses daylight saving. Neighbouring Arizona does not, so for about eight months a year clocks in the two states differ by an hour and for the remaining four they match.

Which rivers drain New Mexico?

The Rio Grande and the Pecos flow to the Gulf of Mexico, the Canadian River reaches the same gulf by way of the Arkansas and Mississippi, and the San Juan and Gila feed the Colorado River and the Gulf of California. The Tularosa Basin, Estancia Basin and Plains of San Agustin are closed basins whose water reaches no sea at all.
