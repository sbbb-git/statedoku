CTConnecticut Geography

Capital: Hartford · Northeast · Admitted 1788

The highest ground in Connecticut is not the top of a mountain. It is a spot at 2,380 feet on the south shoulder of Mount Frissell, whose actual summit stands a short walk north across the Massachusetts line. The tallest peak whose summit lies inside Connecticut is Bear Mountain in Salisbury at 2,316 feet, 64 feet lower than the hillside that outranks it. Both sit in the far northwest corner, in the Taconic country where three states converge.

Boundaries settled by argument, not by terrain

Almost none of Connecticut's land borders follow a natural feature, and the reason is a bad survey. In 1642 Massachusetts sent Nathaniel Woodward and Solomon Saffery to fix the southern limit of its charter. Rather than cut a line through the woods they sailed around Cape Cod and up the coast, and the line they reported landed roughly eight miles too far south, taking in territory Connecticut considered its own.

The fight ran for generations. A 1713 settlement let Massachusetts keep the towns already planted and gave Connecticut 107,793 acres of compensation, the so called Equivalent Lands, in country that is now southern Vermont and western Massachusetts. Connecticut auctioned the lot in 1716 for about 683 pounds and handed the proceeds to the young collegiate school that had just taken the name of Elihu Yale.

One piece of the old error survives on every map. The Southwick Jog is a notch of Massachusetts roughly two and a half miles wide that drops about a mile south into the otherwise straight northern boundary, north of Granby. The 1804 compromise that fixed it also split Congamond Lakes, sending the southern basin to Connecticut.

The western line has its own bargain. In 1731 Connecticut kept the panhandle towns of Greenwich and Stamford, which reach southwest toward New York City, and paid for them with the Oblong, a strip 1.81 miles wide running about 50 miles north from Ridgefield to the Massachusetts corner. That is 61,440 acres, and it is why the New York border jogs east partway up.

Even the internal lines are unusual. The eight counties lost every governmental function in 1960 and existed afterward only as survey outlines. In 2022 the Census Bureau approved replacing them, and since the 2024 data year the federal statistical system uses nine councils of governments as Connecticut's county equivalents.

A rift valley that never became an ocean

Down the middle of the state runs a lowland 20 miles wide, the Central Valley or Hartford Basin. It is the scar of a continental rift that began pulling apart about 200 million years ago as Pangaea broke up. The rift stalled. The Atlantic opened further east instead, leaving a half finished basin filled with red sandstone, lake beds and three thick sheets of basalt.

Those lava sheets were later tilted and stripped by erosion, and their upturned edges form the traprock ridges that give the valley its skyline: the Hanging Hills of Meriden, Talcott Mountain above Farmington, East Rock and West Rock at New Haven, and Sleeping Giant in Hamden. Strung together as the Metacomet Ridge they run roughly 100 miles from Long Island Sound into Massachusetts.

The basin's mudstones preserve what walked across them. In 1966 a bulldozer clearing ground for a state building in Rocky Hill exposed about 2,000 dinosaur tracks in a single bedding plane. The site became Dinosaur State Park, and the three toed print called Eubrontes giganteus is now the state fossil. On either side of the valley the ground rises into the Western and Eastern Uplands, both built of far older metamorphic rock.

The longest river in New England, and its missing port

The Connecticut River runs 410 miles from Fourth Connecticut Lake, a pond on the Canadian border in New Hampshire, to Long Island Sound. Only the final 68 miles are inside the state that shares its name, and the watershed drains four states.

A sandbar has blocked its mouth at Old Saybrook since colonial times, shifting with every storm and never deep enough for ocean shipping. The result is a river larger than any other on the New England coast with no port city where it meets the sea. Hartford, 45 miles upstream, became the head of navigation instead, and the undeveloped tidal marshes at the mouth are now protected as a wetland of international importance under the Ramsar convention.

A shoreline with no surf

Connecticut's 96 miles of coast face Long Island Sound, not the open ocean. Long Island itself is a terminal moraine, a ridge of rock and gravel bulldozed into place and abandoned by the Laurentide ice sheet, and it blocks the Atlantic swell completely. Waves on the Connecticut shore are wind chop rather than ocean surf.

The Sound narrows westward, and the tide narrows with it: the range grows from roughly 3 feet at the eastern entrance near Stonington to about 7 feet at Greenwich. The Thames, Housatonic and Quinnipiac all enter as drowned river valleys, flooded when meltwater raised sea level after the glaciers withdrew.

The same ice left drumlins, kettle ponds and a stony soil that frustrated colonial farmers and pushed them toward the fast falling streams that powered the state's early mills. Temperature records span from 106 degrees Fahrenheit at Danbury on July 15, 1995 to minus 32 at Falls Village on February 16, 1943, a range of 138 degrees inside 5,543 square miles, the third smallest state area in the country.

Common questions about Connecticut geography

What is the highest point in Connecticut?

A point at 2,380 feet on the south slope of Mount Frissell in Salisbury. The summit of Frissell itself lies in Massachusetts. Bear Mountain, 2,316 feet, is the highest peak whose summit is inside Connecticut.

Is Connecticut on the Atlantic Ocean?

Its 96 miles of shore front Long Island Sound, an estuary sheltered by Long Island. Open Atlantic swell never reaches it, which is why the Connecticut shoreline has no surf beaches.

Why does Massachusetts stick down into Connecticut at Southwick?

A 1642 survey by Nathaniel Woodward and Solomon Saffery placed the boundary about eight miles too far south. The 1804 settlement left a notch of Southwick, Massachusetts, roughly two and a half miles wide, projecting into Connecticut.

How long is the Connecticut River?

410 miles, from Fourth Connecticut Lake on the Canadian border to Long Island Sound. It is the longest river in New England, though only its last 68 miles lie within Connecticut.

Does Connecticut still have counties?

Its eight counties lost all government functions in 1960. Since the 2024 data year the federal statistical system uses nine councils of governments as the state's county equivalents instead.
