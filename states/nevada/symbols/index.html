NVNevada State Symbols

Capital: Carson City · West · Admitted 1864

Nevada's flag carries a phrase no other state flag has: Battle Born. Congress admitted Nevada on 31 October 1864, eight days before the presidential election, because Republicans wanted its votes and its support for the Thirteenth Amendment. There was no time to mail the state constitution to Washington, so Carson City telegraphed the entire document, more than sixteen thousand words, at a cost of roughly $3,400. The rush is written into the state's identity, and onto its flag.

What is on the Nevada state flag

The field is cobalt blue. In the upper hoist corner sits a silver five-pointed star. Directly below the star is the word Nevada, and directly above it a golden scroll reading Battle Born. Two sprays of sagebrush, the state flower, cross beneath the star to form a half wreath. Silver for the Comstock Lode, sagebrush for the high desert, the scroll for Civil War statehood.

The design came out of a public contest held in 1926 and was adopted in 1929, credited to Louis Shellback III. The original statute scattered the letters N, E, V, A, D and A individually around the star among the sagebrush leaves, which flag makers largely ignored as unworkable. In 1991 the legislature rewrote the law to place the word in a single line below the star, formalising what manufacturers had been producing for sixty years.

The state seal and the mining economy it froze in place

Nevada's seal dates to 1866, two years after statehood. The foreground shows a quartz mill and a miner with an ore car, the machinery of hard-rock silver extraction rather than placer panning. Beside it are a plow, a sheaf of wheat and a sickle for agriculture. In the middle distance a train crosses a trestle bridge and a telegraph line runs alongside it, with the sun rising over the snow-covered Sierra Nevada behind. Thirty-six stars ring the outer border, because Nevada was the thirty-sixth state, and the motto All for Our Country runs beneath them.

Symbols that surprise people

  • The state fossil is a marine reptile. Shonisaurus popularis, adopted in 1977, was an ichthyosaur that grew past fifteen metres. Its remains sit in the Nevada desert at Berlin-Ichthyosaur State Park, from a period when the ground was ocean floor.
  • Nevada has an official state artifact. The tule duck decoy, named in 1995, refers to bulrush decoys recovered from Lovelock Cave that are roughly two thousand years old.
  • Two state trees. The single-leaf pinyon was designated in 1953, and the bristlecone pine was added in 1987 rather than substituted, so both hold the title.
  • Three separate mineral categories. Silver is the state metal, Virgin Valley black fire opal is the precious gemstone, Nevada turquoise is the semiprecious gemstone, and sandstone is the state rock.
  • The state flower is a shrub. Sagebrush, adopted in 1917, is the plant that covers most of the Great Basin, and it appears on the flag as the wreath around the star.

Frequently asked questions

What is the state bird of Nevada?

The mountain bluebird, adopted in 1967. It is also the state bird of Idaho.

What is on the Nevada state flag?

A cobalt blue field with a silver five-pointed star in the upper hoist corner, the word Nevada below the star, a golden scroll reading Battle Born above it, and two crossed sprays of sagebrush forming a half wreath. The design won a 1926 contest and was adopted in 1929.

What is the Nevada state motto?

All for our country. It appears on the state seal, which was adopted in 1866.

What is the state flower of Nevada?

Sagebrush, adopted in 1917. It is the dominant shrub of the Great Basin and appears on the state flag as a wreath around the silver star.

Why is Nevada called the Battle Born State?

Nevada was admitted to the Union on 31 October 1864, during the Civil War. Its constitution was telegraphed from Carson City to Washington to make the deadline. The phrase Battle Born appears on the state flag.
